> [!definition] Working Capital
> Working capital is what a company needs to fund its daily operations: stock and money owed by customers, minus what it owes suppliers and others in the short term.

## Explain

A shop buys goods before it sells them and may let customers pay later. Until cash arrives, its own money is tied up. That tied-up money is working capital.

If a company collects from customers faster than it pays suppliers, working capital can be negative: customers and suppliers are effectively funding the business. Screener shows 'working capital days', the number of days of sales tied up.

> [!formula] Working capital
> $$\text{Working capital} = \text{Current assets} - \text{Current liabilities}$$
> - Current assets: stock, money owed by customers, cash
> - Current liabilities: bills due within a year

## Data: Reliance

> [!in-practice] Reliance Industries in numbers
> - Working capital days: −66 in FY26, −64 in FY15
> - Reached −179 days in FY17 and FY18
> - Debtor days 20, inventory days 88, days payable 84 in FY26

*Source: [Screener.in](https://www.screener.in/company/RELIANCE/consolidated/), Reliance Industries, consolidated, viewed 3 Oct 2026. Amounts in ₹ crore unless stated. Used to show how to read the numbers; this is not investment advice.*

## Example

> [!example] Negative is not bad
> Reliance's working capital days are negative every year shown. The business collects and holds money for suppliers and others longer than it ties up its own cash.
>
> A negative figure usually helps cash flow. The danger is when it turns sharply less negative, as the cash conversion cycle did in FY26 (see [[cash-conversion-cycle|Cash Conversion Cycle]]).
