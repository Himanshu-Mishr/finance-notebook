> [!definition] Cash Conversion Cycle
> The cash conversion cycle (CCC) is debtor days plus inventory days minus days payable. It is how long a company's own cash stays tied up in the cycle.

## Explain

Follow one batch of goods: you buy (and pay later), hold stock, sell on credit and wait for payment. The cycle ends when the cash arrives. The shorter, the better.

A negative CCC means the company collects from customers before it has to pay suppliers, so the cycle funds itself.

> [!formula] CCC
> $$\text{CCC} = \text{Debtor days} + \text{Inventory days} - \text{Days payable}$$
> - Debtor days: see [[debtor-days|Debtor Days]]
> - Inventory days: see [[inventory-days|Inventory Days]]
> - Days payable: see [[days-payable|Days Payable]]

## Data: Reliance

> [!in-practice] Reliance Industries in numbers
> - FY26: 20 + 88 − 84 = 24, shown as 25 by Screener (rounding)
> - FY25: −8; FY17: −38; FY18: −46

*Source: [Screener.in](https://www.screener.in/company/RELIANCE/consolidated/), Reliance Industries, consolidated, viewed 3 Oct 2026. Amounts in ₹ crore unless stated. Used to show how to read the numbers; this is not investment advice.*

## Example

> [!example] A sign worth watching
> For years Reliance's cycle was negative. In FY26 it moved to +25 days, because debtor days rose (16 to 20) and days payable fell (108 to 84).
>
> About 33 days of extra working capital is now funded by the company. On sales per day of about ₹2,892 crore that is on the order of ₹95,000 crore. A reader would ask: one-off timing or a lasting change?

> [!critical] Treat one year with caution
> Year-end figures can be swung by timing, such as a large order arriving just before March. Look at several years.
